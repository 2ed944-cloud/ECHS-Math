A limit at infinity describes an end of the graph

Determine limits as inputs increase or decrease without bound, and relate those limits to horizontal asymptotes and contextual behavior.

In \lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x)=L, the inputs increase without bound while the outputs approach the finite number L. The notation x\to-\infty describes the other end: inputs become increasingly negative. Neither notation refers to an ordinary input called infinity.

If either end has finite limit L, the line y=L is a horizontal asymptote. The two ends can have the same finite limit, different finite limits, or no finite limit. A graph can cross a horizontal asymptote at finite inputs; a horizontal asymptote describes eventual closeness, not a boundary that the graph cannot cross.

Normalize a rational function by a dominant power

Determine limits as inputs increase or decrease without bound, and relate those limits to horizontal asymptotes and contextual behavior.

Divide the numerator and denominator by the highest power of x in the denominator. Terms such as 1/x and 1/x^2 approach zero at either end. If numerator and denominator have equal degree, the end limit is the ratio of their leading coefficients. If the numerator has smaller degree, the end limit is zero.

If the numerator has larger degree, there is no horizontal asymptote; determine the sign and size of the dominant quotient. For example, 2x^3/(x^2+1) behaves like 2x: it increases without bound to the right and decreases without bound to the left. The degree comparison is a shortcut justified by this normalization.

A square root introduces an absolute value

Determine limits as inputs increase or decrease without bound, and relate those limits to horizontal asymptotes and contextual behavior.

For every real x, \sqrt{x^2}=|x|. Thus \sqrt{x^2+9}/x=(|x|/x)\sqrt{1+9/x^2} when x\ne0. At the right end, |x|/x=1; at the left end it equals −1. The limits are therefore 1 and −1, respectively.

Do not replace \sqrt{x^2} with x when inputs tend to -\infty. Rationalization can also be useful for differences of radicals. Explain the sign choice explicitly whenever extracting a squared variable from a radical.

Separate finite-input behavior from end behavior

Determine limits as inputs increase or decrease without bound, and relate those limits to horizontal asymptotes and contextual behavior.

A vertical asymptote concerns inputs approaching a finite number; a horizontal asymptote concerns inputs tending to positive or negative infinity. A function can have holes, vertical asymptotes, and horizontal asymptotes at the same time. Simplification identifies possible holes; one-sided limits identify vertical asymptotes; limits at infinity identify horizontal asymptotes.

For exponential expressions, first consider the exponential’s own behavior. For instance, e^{-x}\to0 as x\to+\infty. Tables and graphs can suggest a long-term value, but finitely many samples do not prove it. In a contextual model, report the limiting output with units and explain what happens as the input grows.

Compare relative growth with a ratio. If positive functions f and g both increase without bound, the limit f(x)/g(x)\to0 means that g grows faster than f: the output of f becomes arbitrarily small relative to the output of g. A ratio tending to +\infty reverses that comparison; a finite positive ratio means the outputs remain comparable in size. For example, given \lim_{x\to+\infty}x/e^x=0, exponential growth outpaces linear growth even though both x and e^x tend to +\infty. A zero ratio does not say that its numerator tends to zero. Interpret a supplied limit directly; no differentiation rule is needed.

One formula, three kinds of behavior

Determine limits as inputs increase or decrease without bound, and relate those limits to horizontal asymptotes and contextual behavior.

Let f(x)=(3x^2-3)/(x^2+x-2), with the original denominator nonzero. Factor to get 3(x-1)(x+1)/[(x-1)(x+2)]. Nearby values agree with 3(x+1)/(x+2), but the original exclusions are 1 and −2.

At 1, the finite limit is 2, so there is a removable discontinuity. At −2, the simplified numerator approaches −3 while the denominator changes sign through zero, giving a vertical asymptote. At either end, 3(x+1)/(x+2)=(3+3/x)/(1+2/x)\to3. Thus y=3 is a horizontal asymptote. Each conclusion uses a different limiting question.

Different horizontal asymptotes from a radical

Determine limits as inputs increase or decrease without bound, and relate those limits to horizontal asymptotes and contextual behavior.

Find both end limits of g(x)=2x/\sqrt{x^2+4}. Extract |x| from the denominator: g(x)=2(x/|x|)/\sqrt{1+4/x^2}.

For positive unbounded inputs, x/|x|=1, and the denominator tends to 1, so \lim_{x\to+\infty}g(x)=2. For negative unbounded inputs, x/|x|=-1, giving \lim_{x\to-\infty}g(x)=-2. The graph has horizontal asymptotes y=2 and y=-2. The denominator is always positive, so no finite-input denominator zero creates a vertical asymptote.
