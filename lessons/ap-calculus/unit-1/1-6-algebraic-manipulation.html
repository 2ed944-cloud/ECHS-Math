Four steps in a complete solution

The form 0/0 is a signal to investigate, never an answer.

1 · DiagnoseTry substitution. Is the reduced denominator nonzero?
2 · RewriteChoose a factorization, conjugate, common denominator, or identity.
3 · RestrictState x\ne c. Cancel only nonzero factors on a nearby domain.
4 · EvaluateApply the limit laws to the equivalent expression and justify the conclusion.

A nonzero numerator over a denominator tending to zero needs side analysis. A removable factor may produce any finite value, including zero. A picture or table supports the reasoning; exact equivalence establishes it.

Cancel factors, preserve the restriction

Write the factorization before the cancellation.

Worked example

Find \lim_{x\to3}(x^2-9)/(x-3).

  1. Substitution gives 0/0.
  2. Factor: x^2-9=(x-3)(x+3).
  3. For x\ne3, the quotient equals x+3.
  4. Thus the limit is 3+3=6.

The original quotient remains undefined at 3.

A cancellation error

\frac{x^2+4}{x+2}\ne x+2.

Addition does not create a cancellable common factor. At x = 2, direct substitution gives 2.

The expressions agree for every sufficiently close x other than 3. This is a punctured neighborhood: the target is excluded. A limit depends on these nearby values.

Use the coefficient sum and product to identify s. The remaining factor may itself tend to zero.

A conjugate exposes the hidden factor

Multiply the whole fraction by 1, then simplify.

Numerator radical

\lim_{x\to4}\frac{\sqrt{x+5}-3}{x-4}.

Multiply by \frac{\sqrt{x+5}+3}{\sqrt{x+5}+3}.

For x ≠ 4, \frac{x-4}{(x-4)(\sqrt{x+5}+3)}=\frac1{\sqrt{x+5}+3}\to\frac16.

The new denominator tends to 6, so substitution is valid.

Denominator radical

\lim_{x\to4}\frac{x-4}{\sqrt x-2}.

Multiply by \frac{\sqrt x+2}{\sqrt x+2}.

For nearby x ≠ 4, \frac{(x-4)(\sqrt x+2)}{x-4}=\sqrt x+2\to4.

The radical domain includes a neighborhood of 4.

Combine first; preserve the minus sign

Complex fractions often hide a simple common factor.

Worked example

Find \lim_{x\to3}\frac{1/x-1/3}{x-3}.

\frac1x-\frac13=\frac{3-x}{3x}.

For x ≠ 3 near 3, \frac{3-x}{3x(x-3)}=-\frac1{3x}.

The limit is -1/9. The restriction x ≠ 0 is also retained, and is automatically satisfied close enough to 3.

Try a shifted denominator

For \lim_{x\to1}\frac{1/(x+1)-1/2}{x-1}, write the numerator over 2(x+1).

\frac1{x+1}-\frac12=\frac{1-x}{2(x+1)}.

For x ≠ 1 near 1, the full quotient becomes -1/[2(x+1)]\to-1/4.

Use exact trigonometric forms

These core examples use identities followed by continuity.

Three useful identities

1-\cos^2x=\sin^2x.

\tan x=\sin x/\cos x.

\sin(2x)=2\sin x\cos x.

Near 0, sine is nonzero away from 0 and cosine stays nonzero, so the indicated cancellations are valid on a sufficiently small punctured neighborhood.

Worked example

\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\tan x}{\sin x}=\lim_{x\to0}\frac1{\cos x}=1.

The canceled factor is \sin x, not x. We used an exact identity before taking the limit.

The harder sine-anchor and third-order cancellation questions are retained after AP practice. Their supplied anchor uses radians; its squeeze-theorem proof is a Topic 1.8 connection.

Write a complete algebraic justification

Keep the equality statements valid at every step.

Strong response

For x ≠ 2, \frac{x^2+x-6}{x-2}=x+3. Since these expressions agree for all sufficiently close non-target inputs, their limits at 2 are equal. By the polynomial limit law, the limit is 5.

Check your reasoning

  • Does the factorization expand back to the original?
  • Did you cancel a factor rather than a term?
  • Did you retain the excluded target and any radical restrictions?
  • Does the final denominator have a nonzero limit?
  • Have you distinguished the function value from the limit?
