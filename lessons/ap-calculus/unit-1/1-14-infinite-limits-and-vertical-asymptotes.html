Infinity describes behavior, not an assigned value

Determine infinite limits from formulas, graphs, and tables, and use them to identify vertical asymptotes.

The notation \lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=+\infty says that as inputs approach a from the right, outputs eventually exceed any chosen positive bound. It does not say that f(a) is an infinite real number. Likewise, a limit of -\infty means outputs decrease without bound.

Keep the input direction and output behavior separate: x\to a^+ describes where the inputs are; f(x)\to-\infty describes the outputs. An infinite limit is not a finite real limit, but its signed notation communicates more information than simply writing “DNE.”

A sign chart supplies the missing direction

Determine infinite limits from formulas, graphs, and tables, and use them to identify vertical asymptotes.

Substitution that gives a nonzero numerator over zero signals that sign analysis is needed. Factor the denominator and examine each factor just to the left and right of the target. A squared factor is positive on both sides; an odd power changes sign when its base changes sign. Also track the numerator and any nonvanishing factors.

For example, -2/(x-3)^2 tends to -\infty from both sides of 3. In contrast, -2/(x-3) tends to +\infty from the left and -\infty from the right. “The denominator goes to zero” is not a complete sign justification.

One unbounded side is enough for a vertical asymptote

Determine infinite limits from formulas, graphs, and tables, and use them to identify vertical asymptotes.

The line x=a is a vertical asymptote if at least one of the one-sided limits at a is +\infty or -\infty. Both sides need not have the same sign, and a function may approach a vertical asymptote from only one side of its domain.

If both one-sided limits are +\infty, write the two-sided infinite limit as +\infty; use the analogous notation for -\infty. If the sides have opposite signs, the two-sided limit does not exist, even though a vertical asymptote is present. Assigning a finite value at the asymptote does not alter this nearby behavior.

Check cancellation and the limits of evidence

Determine infinite limits from formulas, graphs, and tables, and use them to identify vertical asymptotes.

A zero denominator is a candidate to inspect, not an automatic asymptote. Factor first when the numerator also tends to zero. The expression (x^2-4)/(x-2) has a hole at 2 because it equals x+2 nearby, with finite limit 4. The expression (x^2-4)/(x-2)^2 retains one denominator factor and is unbounded there.

Graphs and tables can suggest signed infinite limits, but a finite viewing window or sample list cannot prove behavior arbitrarily close to the target. Use an exact formula, stated limiting behavior, or fully specified graph features to justify a definitive conclusion.

Separate a hole from a vertical asymptote

Determine infinite limits from formulas, graphs, and tables, and use them to identify vertical asymptotes.

Let R(x)=(x^2-9)/[(x-3)(x+1)]. For x\ne3,-1, factoring gives R(x)=(x+3)/(x+1). At 3, the limit is 6/4=3/2, so the excluded point produces a hole.

At −1, the numerator approaches 2, which is positive. From the left the denominator is negative and approaches zero, so \lim_{x\to-1^-}R(x)=-\infty. From the right it is positive, so \lim_{x\to-1^+}R(x)=+\infty. Therefore x=-1 is a vertical asymptote and the two-sided limit at −1 does not exist. The original domain exclusions remain relevant even after cancellation.

Use an even power and a nonzero factor

Determine infinite limits from formulas, graphs, and tables, and use them to identify vertical asymptotes.

Find the one-sided limits of f(x)=(x-4)/[(x-1)^2(x+2)] at 1. Near 1, the numerator is negative and approaches −3. The factor x+2 is positive and approaches 3. The squared factor is positive on each side and approaches zero.

Thus the denominator approaches zero through positive values on both sides, while the numerator remains negative and nonzero. Both one-sided limits are -\infty. We may write \lim_{x\to1}f(x)=-\infty, and x=1 is a vertical asymptote. This conclusion comes from sign and magnitude, not from treating division by zero as arithmetic.
