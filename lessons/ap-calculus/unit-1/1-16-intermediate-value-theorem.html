State all hypotheses before the conclusion

Apply the Intermediate Value Theorem with complete hypotheses and a precise existence conclusion, including in contextual and multiple-representation problems.

The Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT) says that if f is continuous on [a,b] and N lies strictly between f(a) and f(b), then there is at least one c\in(a,b) with f(c)=N. The endpoint values may increase or decrease; either ordering works.

A complete application names the continuous function and closed interval, gives the endpoint values, compares the target to those values, and states the existence conclusion in the open interval. If the target equals an endpoint value, an endpoint already supplies a solution, but an interior solution need not exist.

Prove a root without finding it

Apply the Intermediate Value Theorem with complete hypotheses and a precise existence conclusion, including in contextual and multiple-representation problems.

To show that an equation f(x)=0 has a solution, it is often enough to find opposite signs at two endpoints where f is continuous throughout the interval. For example, p(x)=x^3+x-1 is polynomial and continuous on [0,1], with p(0)=-1\lt 0\lt p(1)=1. The IVT guarantees at least one root in (0,1).

The argument neither computes the root nor proves it is unique. To study f(x)=g(x), apply the same reasoning to h(x)=f(x)-g(x), which is continuous when both functions are continuous on the interval.

Use tables and graphs with stated continuity

Apply the Intermediate Value Theorem with complete hypotheses and a precise existence conclusion, including in contextual and multiple-representation problems.

A table gives exact values at selected inputs. With continuity stated on the entire interval, endpoint pairs that bracket a target support an IVT argument between those inputs. Three disjoint open intervals with such bracketing give at least three distinct solutions. Count intervals carefully; overlapping intervals may contain the same solution.

A graph of a continuous curve can support the same comparison. A graph with a hole, jump, or vertical asymptote inside the interval does not meet the IVT hypothesis on that interval. Continuity must hold everywhere between the endpoints, not merely at the endpoints or at the table’s sampled inputs.

Know the scope of the guarantee

Apply the Intermediate Value Theorem with complete hypotheses and a precise existence conclusion, including in contextual and multiple-representation problems.

Opposite signs do not guarantee a zero without continuity. The function 1/x has opposite signs at −1 and 1 but never equals zero, and it is not continuous on [-1,1]. Conversely, equal-sign endpoint values do not prove that a continuous function has no roots; the graph could dip below zero between them.

Use “at least one” unless additional information establishes uniqueness. The IVT does not assume a straight-line change, so it does not identify a midpoint solution or justify linear interpolation as exact. In a model, attach units to the input and target and explain the guaranteed event in context.

Three intervals, three distinct events

Apply the Intermediate Value Theorem with complete hypotheses and a precise existence conclusion, including in contextual and multiple-representation problems.

A temperature T is continuous for 0\le t\le6. It has values T(0)=18, T(2)=24, T(4)=20, and T(6)=25. On [0,2], 18\lt 22\lt 24, so the IVT gives a time in (0,2) at which the temperature is 22.

On [2,4], the endpoint values 24 and 20 also bracket 22, giving a time in (2,4). On [4,6], 20 and 25 bracket 22, giving a time in (4,6). Because these open intervals are disjoint, the times are distinct. We have proved at least three times, with no claim that there are exactly three.

Repair the function before applying the theorem

Apply the Intermediate Value Theorem with complete hypotheses and a precise existence conclusion, including in contextual and multiple-representation problems.

Let F(x)=(x^2-4)/(x-2) for 0\le x\lt 2, F(2)=k, and F(x)=x^2 for 2\lt x\le3. The left branch equals x+2 nearby, so the left limit at 2 is 4. The right limit is also 4. Assigning k=4 makes the join continuous, and each branch is continuous elsewhere.

Now F is continuous on [2,3], with F(2)=4\lt 6\lt 9=F(3). The IVT guarantees a c\in(2,3) with F(c)=6. Checking the join was necessary to justify using this closed interval. The theorem proves existence without needing to solve c^2=6.
