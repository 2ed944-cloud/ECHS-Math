The three central ideas

  1. An average rate needs a nonzero interval.

  3. The limit idea connects secant slopes to a tangent slope.

Start with a quotient of changes

Calculate an average rate and interpret it as the slope of a secant line.

For distinct inputs a and b, the average rate of change is

\frac{\text{change in output}}{\text{change in input}}=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}

The numerator has output units. The denominator has input units. Their quotient has output units per input unit.

Keep the subtraction order consistent in the numerator and denominator.

Worked example

Let f(x)=x^2+1. Find the average rate on [1,3].

1. Evaluate: f(1)=2, f(3)=10.
2. Subtract: output change 10-2=8; input change 3-1=2.
3. Divide: 8/2=4.
4. Interpret: on this interval the output increases by an average of 4 units for each input unit.

Why one point is not enough

Explain why the average-rate formula is undefined when its interval collapses.

A tempting substitution

Setting the two inputs equal gives

\frac{f(a)-f(a)}{a-a}=\frac{0}{0}

This quotient is undefined. It is not zero and is not a number called infinity.

The question is not “What is this quotient at zero width?” It is “What do the rates approach as the width becomes smaller?”

The same point can belong to different motions

At t=2, both A(t)=2t+5 and B(t)=t^2+5 have position 9 metres.

On [2,2.01], their average velocities are 2 m/s and 4.01 m/s.

A position value alone cannot tell us how quickly position is changing. We need information nearby.

An instantaneous rate can be nonzero even though the average-rate quotient at identical endpoints is undefined.

Why these rates approach 22.4

Connect numerical evidence to a simple algebraic expression.

Use a second time 0.7+h, with h\ne0.

\frac{16(0.7+h)^2-16(0.7)^2}{h}
Expand: the numerator is 22.4h+16h^2.
Factor: h(22.4+16h).
Divide for h ≠ 0: the quotient equals 22.4+16h.
Approach: as h approaches 0, the rate approaches 22.4 ft/s.

The limit idea

Keep the interval nonzero and ask what common value the average rates approach.

\frac{s(0.7+h)-s(0.7)}{h}\longrightarrow22.4\quad\text{as }h\to0

The position graph has instantaneous downward velocity 22.4 ft/s at 0.7 seconds.

A preview of limit notation
\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}

Read this as “the value approached by the average rates as the interval width approaches zero.” When the limit is finite and exists, it is the instantaneous rate at c. Formal limit notation is the focus of Lesson 1.2.

Exact calculation or supported estimate?

Separate an interval rate from an instantaneous rate inferred from finite data.

What is exact from these readings?

The quotient (113.2 − 117)/2 is exactly −1.9 for the stated numbers. It gives the average rate on [29, 31].

Using −1.9 for the instantaneous rate at 30 seconds is an estimate.

Different smooth models can pass through the same finite measurements and behave differently between them.

Good AP reasoning habits

  1. Name the interval used for the estimate.
  2. Show the quotient before evaluating.
  3. Give output units per input unit.
  4. Use “approximately” when the data justify an estimate.
  5. Explain the time, sign and meaning in context.

A smaller interval can improve an estimate for a suitable smooth model. Rounded readings can also make very small differences unreliable.

Read a rate in its own context

State what changes, at which instant, in what direction and with which units.

A complete interpretation

“At 30 seconds after exercise, the heart-rate reading is decreasing by approximately 1.9 beats/minute each second.”

Check the domain too

A record of total free throws after completed games is defined at integer game numbers. It does not automatically define a value at 49.999 games.

An instantaneous rate requires an appropriate model defined at nearby inputs. A continuous model must be introduced explicitly when the original measurements are discrete.

Sign describes direction

A negative position rate means motion in the chosen negative direction. The corresponding speed is its nonnegative magnitude.

Turn calculator output into an explanation

Distinguish a model value, an average rate and an estimated instantaneous rate.

For V(t)=92-15\sin(t/3), the input is minutes and the output is a modelled number of vehicles in a departure lane.

1. What does V(18) describe?

2. What does the average rate on [15, 20] describe?

3. What do the nearby rates suggest at 18 minutes?

Try the model explorer before revealing the worked solution.

Model value: V(18)\approx96.191 vehicles.
Interval average: \dfrac{V(20)-V(15)}5\approx-3.999 vehicles/min.
Nearby rates: [17.99, 18] gives about −4.799; [18, 18.01] gives about −4.803 vehicles/min.
Instantaneous estimate: about −4.801 vehicles/min. At 18 minutes, the modelled lane count is decreasing at roughly this rate.

The formula is a continuous approximation to a discrete count. Its noninteger outputs are model predictions.

Challenge: can a centred quotient mislead?

Test a proposed instantaneous rate against approaches from both sides.

Let p(t)=|t-2|. A student uses symmetric intervals about 2 and always obtains zero.

The maroon lines follow the two sides of the corner. The horizontal dashed chord joins equally high points on opposite sides.

Make and test a claim

Does the centred rate prove that the instantaneous velocity at 2 is zero? Explain what happens from each side.

For every positive h, the centred quotient is 0 because the endpoint positions are equal.

The left-interval rate is −1; the right-interval rate is +1. They never approach a common value, so this model has no single instantaneous velocity at 2.

A centred quotient alone can hide disagreement between the two sides.

Challenge: the same table, different local rates

Explain why finite values cannot uniquely determine an instantaneous rate.

Two models are

f(t)=t^2
g(t)=t^2+10(t-1)(t-2)(t-3)

Both give the table (1, 1), (2, 4), (3, 9).

1. Explain why those three values match.

2. With t = 2 + h, simplify each average-rate quotient from 2 to 2 + h.

3. What values do the quotients approach as h approaches 0?

At t = 1, 2 or 3, one factor in the added product is zero.

For f, the quotient is 4+h.

For g, the added numerator at 2 + h is 10(1+h)h(h-1). After division by nonzero h, the full quotient becomes

4+h+10(h^2-1)=-6+h+10h^2

The rates approach 4 and −6 respectively. Identical finite table values do not force identical local rates.

This extension uses algebra and the limit idea; no differentiation rules are needed.

Challenge: repair the argument

Find the exact step where a plausible explanation breaks down.

Argument A

“The cyclist’s average velocity is 162.5 m/min, so the average speed must also be 162.5 m/min.”

Velocity uses displacement, while speed uses total distance. The return segment makes those numerators different. The correct average speed is 237.5 m/min.

Argument B

“The tangent at the origin to y=x^3 cannot be horizontal because the graph crosses that line.”

The secant slope from the origin to (h,h^3) is h^2, for h ≠ 0. Those slopes approach 0 from either side. The horizontal line is tangent even though the curve crosses it.

From average change to change at an instant

Bring the graph, the table, the formula and the context together.

The central connection

\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}

A secant slope describes change over a nonzero interval. As the interval shrinks around an instant, a common finite value approached by the rates gives the instantaneous rate there.

That limiting value is the tangent slope. It has output units per input unit and must be interpreted in context.
